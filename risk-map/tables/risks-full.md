| riskOverScopedToolAuthority                | Over-Scoped Tool Authority                                 | A tool exposed to an agent with broader authority than any single task requires, whose constraints are enforced by the model's own judgment rather than deterministically at the tool boundary, lets a manipulated or merely over-reaching model exercise that full excess authority — collapsing the system's effective security to the model's weakest behavior.<br>                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Tools and capabilities exposed to an agent are designed with authority broader than any individual task requires, and the decision to constrain that authority — input validation, parameter bounds, object-level authorization, and policy enforcement — is delegated to the model's reasoning rather than enforced deterministically at the tool boundary. The model is made the security enforcement point. Because the model is non-deterministic and manipulable, the effective security of the system collapses to the weakest model behavior, and even robust upstream authentication and authorization controls can be undermined by the breadth of what the tool is permitted to do once invoked.<br><br> This is a classical least-privilege and secure-by-design failure in agentic dress ([Model Context Protocol Security: Secure Tool and UX Design](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/draft/basic/security_best_practices)). The same pattern recurs as over-privileged service accounts holding ambient authority, clients trusted to enforce access control that belongs on the server, and broken object-level authorization (BOLA) where the caller is trusted to scope its own request. Reference access-control architecture separates the policy enforcement point (PEP), decision point (PDP), and information point (PIP) ([NIST SP 800-162: Guide to Attribute Based Access Control (ABAC) Definition and Considerations](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/162/upd2/final)) precisely so that enforcement does not depend on the requesting actor's good behavior. Delegating enforcement to the LLM collapses these into a single untrusted actor. The AI-specific amplifiers are that the agent autonomously discovers, plans, and chains tool invocations, so a single over-scoped tool can be composed into actions its designer never anticipated, and that the model's susceptibility to manipulated input (see Prompt Injection) turns a held over-grant into an externally triggerable one.<br><br> The exposure persists wherever a tool holds standing capability beyond least privilege, wherever a tool's constraints are expressed as model instructions rather than as deterministic checks enforced before the action executes, and wherever the granted authority is coarse-grained or long-lived rather than ephemeral and context-scoped per action. The trigger is mechanism-independent: the over-grant is realized by adversarial manipulation of the model, by ambiguity in benign tasks, or simply by the agent operating within its intended-but-too-broad parameters.<br><br> This risk is distinct from Rogue Actions, which is the output/action-layer manifestation — the unintended or manipulated action actually executed via an extension. Over-Scoped Tool Authority is the upstream design condition that sets the blast radius of any such action; it is present and exploitable even when no rogue action has yet occurred, and it is mitigated by design-time controls (scope narrowing, relocation of enforcement out of the model) rather than action-time controls. It is also broader than Agentic Delegation Confused Deputy, which requires asymmetric privilege across a caller/deputy delegation boundary; the over-grant here is a property of a single tool's design and needs no second principal.<br><br> Potential impacts include unauthorized data access and exfiltration, destructive or financial actions executed at the tool's full authority, and privilege escalation as over-scoped tools are chained. Because enforcement was delegated to a non-deterministic model, these consequences are hard to bound in advance and degrade accountability for which constraint was supposed to stop the action.<br> A process running in cleartext memory has always been exposed to whatever sits inside its host's privilege boundary — the OS, hypervisor, a hardware operator, an insider, or a co-tenant workload that breaks out of its own confinement. Two distinct control tiers close this gap for different adversaries: OS- and process-level isolation (containers, namespaces, seccomp, mandatory access control) bounds what a compromised or escaping co-tenant process can reach, while hardware-enforced confidential computing (TEEs, secure enclaves) removes the host operator and privileged insiders from the trust boundary entirely — a threat OS-level isolation alone cannot address, since an operator with hypervisor-level access sits outside any software boundary.
